Refugees and Migration in Tripura

A historical account of displacement, migration, rehabilitation, and the changing society of Tripura, with attention to the experiences of both displaced families and Indigenous Tiprasa communities.

1. Introduction: Tripura and Migration

The history of migration into Tripura is closely connected with its geographical location, its former princely state, the Partition of British India in 1947, and the Bangladesh Liberation War of 1971. These events reshaped population patterns and affected communities across the state.

Tripura shares a long international boundary with Bangladesh. Across different periods, people crossed this region for trade, employment, family connections, settlement, and refuge from political violence or insecurity. Not every migrant had the same circumstances, legal status, or experience.

Understanding this history requires listening to different experiences: the suffering of displaced families, the hospitality and difficulties of host communities, and the concerns of Indigenous peoples whose relationships with ancestral land, language and customary life were affected by demographic change.

2. Migration Before the Partition of 1947

Migration into the territory of Tripura did not begin in 1947. During the period of the Manikya rulers, people from neighbouring regions moved into the state for a range of reasons, including administration, agriculture, trade, and other forms of work.

The royal administration's policies and the wider political and economic circumstances influenced settlement over time. Historical records also refer to people seeking shelter in Tripura during periods of communal tension in neighbouring areas before Independence.

These earlier movements should be distinguished from the much larger displacement associated with Partition and the 1971 war. Their causes, scale and historical circumstances were not identical.

3. The Partition of 1947 and Its Aftermath

In August 1947, British India was partitioned into India and Pakistan. East Bengal became part of Pakistan and was later known as East Pakistan. It became the independent country of Bangladesh in 1971.

Partition was followed by violence, insecurity, fear, and the displacement of many people. Some Hindu families left East Bengal for India, including Tripura. Migration continued in different waves over the following years, influenced by communal violence, uncertainty, family connections and economic circumstances.

Refugees arrived with different resources and experiences. Some found shelter with relatives or local residents; others depended on government relief and rehabilitation arrangements. Access to housing, cultivable land, employment, education and legal documentation could be difficult.

Historical context: The post-Partition movement was not a single event completed in 1947. Migration continued through subsequent years, and its effects on Tripura's society unfolded over decades.

4. The Bangladesh Liberation War of 1971

In March 1971, military repression and the outbreak of war in East Pakistan triggered a mass flight of civilians into India. Tripura's proximity to East Pakistan made it a major destination for people seeking safety.

Refugees arrived from areas including Comilla, Noakhali, Sylhet and Chittagong. Many experienced fear, family separation, loss of homes and possessions, and uncertainty about their future. Tripura's residents and authorities faced enormous demands for shelter, food, medical care and other assistance.

About 1.38 million Refugees recorded in Tripura in a Government of India count dated 15 December 1971.
276 camps Refugee camps recorded in Tripura in the same historical account.

These figures describe the exceptional emergency of 1971; they are not present-day population figures. Historical totals and reporting dates differ across sources, so the date and source should always accompany the numbers.

After Bangladesh became independent, many displaced people returned. Others remained in India. Their later circumstances varied according to family ties, resettlement, documentation, citizenship and government policy.

Source for the figures: historical refugee statistics reproduced in research on the 1971 refugee crisis. See the academic and archival references below.

5. Effects on Indigenous Tiprasa Communities

The large population movements of the twentieth century changed Tripura's demographic balance and placed pressure on land, employment, public services and political institutions. These changes were particularly significant for Indigenous Tiprasa communities, whose historical ties to land, customary institutions, language and cultural life are central to their identity.

Scholars have examined how demographic change, land pressures, political representation and cultural concerns contributed to tensions and ethnic movements in Tripura. The causes and consequences were complex and developed over time.

It is important to distinguish historical processes and government policies from the conduct of individual people. Refugees were not a single uniform group, just as Indigenous communities included people with differing experiences and perspectives.

  • Land and livelihood: changes in settlement and land use created serious concerns for many communities.
  • Language and culture: the preservation of Kokborok and Indigenous traditions became important public issues.
  • Political representation: demographic change influenced debates about participation, autonomy and governance.
  • Social relations: experiences included mutual assistance as well as conflict, insecurity and competing claims.

6. Rehabilitation, Citizenship and Society

Refugee rehabilitation involved difficult questions of shelter, land allocation, employment, public expenditure and long-term settlement. The experiences of displaced people differed according to when they arrived, where they settled, and what assistance or documentation they received.

Refugee status, migration history and citizenship are distinct matters. They should not be treated as interchangeable. An individual's legal status depends on applicable law and the evidence relevant to that person; it cannot be determined solely from ethnicity, language, religion or place of origin.

The long-term history of Tripura includes the work of people rebuilding their lives, host communities responding to sudden needs, and Indigenous communities seeking to protect their land, identity and rights. A balanced historical account should recognise these experiences without erasing any of them.

7. Oral Histories and Family Memories

Official records and academic studies are essential, but family memories can also help explain how migration affected everyday life. Interviews with elders may preserve information about journeys, temporary shelters, settlement, lost homes, local assistance and changes in village life.

When recording family or community history:

  • Ask permission before recording or publishing a person's story.
  • Identify whether an account is a personal memory, a family tradition or a claim confirmed by documentary evidence.
  • Record dates and locations only when the narrator agrees and the information is reliable.
  • Protect private details and avoid publishing sensitive documents without consent.
  • Respect the dignity of displaced families and Indigenous communities alike.

8. A Shared Responsibility to Remember

The history of refugees and migration in Tripura is a history of human displacement, survival, resettlement and social change. It is also a history of difficult questions about land, belonging, cultural continuity and political representation.

Remembering the past responsibly means preserving historical evidence, acknowledging suffering, respecting Indigenous heritage, and avoiding collective blame. Accurate records and open-minded research can help future generations understand how Tripura became the society it is today.
